<p>Graphic design in Toronto is priced per project or per hour, and there is no official price list for the city. The most reliable public number is what designers here are paid. Job Bank, the Government of Canada's labour market site, lists a median wage of <strong>$33.65 an hour</strong> for graphic designers in the Toronto Region, with the low end at $21.00 and the high end at $53.37 (2023 to 2024 data, updated November 2025).</p>

<p>That is a wage, not a price. A freelancer or studio that bills you also has to cover software, equipment, revisions, client calls and the hours that never get billed, so the rate on a quote is higher than the wage. What you end up paying comes down to two things: the rate, and how many hours the job really takes. This guide covers what moves both, and how to compare two quotes fairly.</p>

<p>The spread matters. A designer at the high end usually has years of experience, a strong portfolio and a specialty such as branding or packaging. A designer at the low end is often early in their career. Neither is automatically the right choice; it depends on the job.</p>

<h2>What changes the price of a design job</h2>

<p>Most quotes come down to hours multiplied by a rate. These are the things that add hours:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Scope.</strong> A single social media graphic is a short job. A brand identity with a logo, colour palette, fonts and usage rules is many smaller jobs in one.</li>
<li><strong>Concepts and revisions.</strong> Three first concepts and two rounds of changes take more time than one concept and one round. Ask how many of each the quote covers.</li>
<li><strong>Research.</strong> A logo for a new business usually needs time spent on competitors and the audience before any drawing starts.</li>
<li><strong>Print or screen.</strong> Print work needs exact sizes, bleed, colour set up for the printer and sometimes a proof. Screen work often needs several sizes for different platforms.</li>
<li><strong>Copy and photos.</strong> If the designer also writes the text or sources the images, that time is in the price.</li>
<li><strong>Rush timelines.</strong> Work that has to jump ahead of other projects often costs more.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Freelancer, agency or design contest</h2>

<p><strong>A freelancer</strong> often has the lowest rate, and you deal directly with the person doing the work. That works well for a single, well-defined job. The risk is availability: if that one person gets busy or sick, your project waits.</p>

<p><strong>An agency</strong> charges more in exchange for a team, a project manager, and the ability to carry the same look into your website, ads and social media. That is useful when design is tied to other work, such as a new logo that also has to go on a new website.</p>

<p><strong>Online contests and marketplaces</strong> give you many options quickly at a set price. The trade-off is less research into your business. Before you pay, check who owns the final files and whether the design is original.</p>

<h2>How to compare two design quotes</h2>

<p>Two quotes for "a logo" can describe very different amounts of work. Ask each designer the same questions:</p>

<ul>
<li>How many first concepts and how many revision rounds are included?</li>
<li>Which files will I receive, for example vector files for the logo, print-ready PDFs, and PNG and JPG files for the web?</li>
<li>Do I own the final artwork and the copyright once the invoice is paid?</li>
<li>Is printing included, or only the print-ready file?</li>
<li>Is HST included in the price?</li>
<li>What is the timeline, and what happens if I need changes after it?</li>
</ul>

<p>A lower quote that leaves out source files or limits you to one revision can end up costing more once you pay for the missing parts later.</p>
