<p>A dentist's website has one job most owners underestimate: turning a nervous, price-sensitive, comparison-shopping visitor into a booked appointment before they close the tab. Patients rarely browse a dental site for fun — they land on it with a specific question (does this practice take my insurance, do they do emergency visits, are they open Saturdays) and decide within seconds whether to keep reading or hit the back button.</p>

<p>That means a dental website has fundamentally different priorities than a typical brochure site. A restaurant site can get away with being mostly photos and a menu PDF. A dental site is competing for a purchase decision that involves pain, cost, and trust all at once — and it's usually being compared against three or four other practices' websites open in other browser tabs at the same time. Here's what actually moves the needle, and what most dental sites get wrong.</p>

<h2>1. Make booking the easiest thing on the page</h2>

<p>The single biggest conversion killer on dental websites is forcing every visitor to call during business hours. A visible "Book an Appointment" button in the header, repeated at the bottom of every service page, removes that friction. It doesn't need to be a complex real-time scheduling system on day one — a simple request form that lands in the front desk's inbox is a massive improvement over a phone number alone, especially for the after-hours and new-patient traffic that finds the site outside office hours.</p>

<h2>2. Real photos beat stock photos every time</h2>

<p>Stock photography of generic smiling models is one of the fastest ways to make a dental site feel untrustworthy. Real photos of the actual office, the actual team, and (with consent) real patient results build the trust that a dental visit — an inherently anxiety-inducing purchase for many people — depends on. If budget only stretches to one professional photography session, spend it here before anything else.</p>

<h2>3. Structure service pages around what patients actually search</h2>

<p>Most dental sites bury every service — cleanings, whitening, implants, emergency care, Invisalign — under one generic "Services" page. That's a missed opportunity twice over: patients researching a specific procedure want a page that speaks directly to their situation, and a single thin services page gives Google far less to rank than dedicated pages for each high-intent search like "emergency dentist" or "dental implants" plus the practice's city. Each service deserves its own page with what it involves, what it costs (even a range), and a direct path to book.</p>

<h2>4. Local SEO is not optional</h2>

<p>Very few patients search a dentist by name — they search "dentist near me" or "[service] + city." A dental website that isn't built around <a href="/services/seo">local SEO</a> — city-specific content, an optimized Google Business Profile, consistent citations across directories — is effectively invisible to the exact patients closest to the door, regardless of how good the design looks. This is the single most common gap between dental sites that fill the schedule and ones that just sit there looking professional.</p>

<h2>5. Speed and mobile experience aren't cosmetic</h2>

<p>Most dental site traffic is mobile — someone searching in pain, on their phone, right now. A slow-loading site with a booking form that's awkward to fill out on a small screen loses exactly the highest-intent visitors: the ones who need an appointment today. Fast load times and a mobile-first booking flow directly affect how many of those visitors actually convert, and Google's ranking algorithm weighs page speed as well.</p>

<h2>6. Reviews need to be visible, not just collected</h2>

<p>Reviews matter twice for a dental practice — they build trust with visitors deciding whether to book, and they're a ranking signal for local search. A dental site that surfaces real reviews directly on the homepage and service pages (rather than linking off to a third-party page) keeps that trust signal where it does the most good, right next to the booking button.</p>

<h2>7. Answer the insurance question before it's asked</h2>

<p>For most patients, "do you take my insurance" and "do you offer direct billing" are decided before they ever pick up the phone — if the site doesn't answer it, they move to the next practice's site that does. A clear, plainly worded insurance and payment section (which providers you direct-bill, whether you accept a range of major Canadian insurers, and what happens if a patient doesn't have coverage) removes one of the most common reasons a visitor leaves without booking. It doesn't need a full breakdown of every plan — just enough clarity that the patient isn't left guessing.</p>

<h2>8. Multi-location and group practices need a locations structure, not one page per city crammed together</h2>

<p>Practices with more than one location often default to a single "Locations" page listing every address in a table. That works for a visitor who already knows which office they want, but it does nothing for search — Google needs a dedicated page per location, each with its own address, hours, team, and city-specific content, to rank each office for its own local searches. A group practice with three locations and one shared locations page is effectively only visible in search for one of those three cities.</p>

<h2>9. Accessibility isn't optional in Ontario</h2>

<p>Ontario businesses — including dental practices — have obligations under the <a href="https://www.ontario.ca/page/accessibility-laws" target="_blank" rel="noreferrer noopener">Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act (AODA)</a> to make their websites usable for people with disabilities: proper heading structure, alt text on images, sufficient colour contrast, and keyboard navigation, among other requirements. Beyond the legal obligation, an accessible site is a better-performing one — the same structural discipline that makes a page usable with a screen reader also tends to make it easier for Google to understand and rank.</p>

<h2>Common mistakes we see in dental website redesigns</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>A phone number as the only way to book</strong> — loses every visitor who found the site outside business hours or would rather not call.</li>
<li><strong>One generic services page</strong> — instead of dedicated pages per procedure that can each rank for their own searches.</li>
<li><strong>No local SEO strategy</strong> — a beautifully designed site that never shows up for "[service] near me" searches isn't generating new patients.</li>
<li><strong>Stock photography throughout</strong> — undermines the trust a dental visit purchase decision depends on.</li>
<li><strong>Slow mobile load times</strong> — directly costs bookings from the highest-intent visitors: people searching in pain, right now, on their phone.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What a redesign should preserve</h2>

<p>Practices switching from an old site are often (understandably) most worried about losing what's already working — existing rankings, backlinks from local directories, and any traffic the current site already gets. A well-planned redesign carries over the URLs that already rank (or 301-redirects them cleanly to their new location), keeps the Google Business Profile and citation data consistent, and treats the migration as an upgrade to the existing foundation rather than a rebuild from zero. Losing existing search visibility during a redesign is avoidable — it just has to be planned for up front, not discovered after launch.</p>
