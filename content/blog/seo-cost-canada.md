<p>"How much does SEO cost" is one of the most searched questions in the industry, and for good reason — pricing varies more in SEO than in almost any other type of marketing spend, and it's genuinely hard to tell a fair quote from an inflated one without understanding what actually drives the cost. This is a straightforward breakdown of what affects SEO pricing in Canada, without the vague "it depends" non-answer that most of these articles land on.</p>

<h2>The honest answer: it depends, but here's why</h2>

<p>SEO pricing across the industry varies enormously — from a few hundred dollars a month for a single-location business in a low-competition market, up into the thousands per month for multi-location businesses competing in crowded industries like law, real estate, or home services in major cities. Multiple independent industry pricing guides published in 2026 place typical small-business SEO in Canada somewhere in the range of roughly $500 to $2,500 per month, with more competitive, larger-scope engagements running higher. That's a wide range on purpose — the honest answer really does depend on your specific situation, and the rest of this article is about what actually moves the number within that range.</p>

<h2>What actually drives the price</h2>

<p>A handful of factors explain most of the variation in SEO quotes:</p>

<p><strong>Competitiveness of your industry and keywords.</strong> Ranking a local bakery for "bakery [city name]" is a fundamentally different project than ranking a personal injury law firm for "car accident lawyer [city name]" — the second one has far more competitors actively investing in SEO, which means more work (content, links, technical optimization) is required to compete. Our own keyword data reflects this directly: a term like "web design calgary" runs a moderate difficulty score, while a broader, more competitive term in a bigger market can run significantly higher. Higher-difficulty keywords cost more to rank for, full stop.</p>

<p><strong>How many locations or service areas you're targeting.</strong> A single-location business needs one strong, well-optimized presence. A business serving ten cities needs ten well-built, genuinely differentiated location pages plus the local SEO work (citations, Google Business Profile, local content) to support each one — which scales the labour involved roughly with the number of markets.</p>

<p><strong>Starting point.</strong> A brand-new website with no existing authority, no citations, and no content history costs more to get moving than an established site with years of accumulated backlinks and rankings that just needs optimization and maintenance. Agencies sometimes quote a higher rate for the first few months of a new-site engagement specifically because more foundational work is required upfront.</p>

<p><strong>What's actually included.</strong> "SEO" as a line item can mean wildly different things — some retainers include content writing, technical audits, link building, and local citation management; others are limited to basic on-page tweaks and a monthly report. Comparing two quotes without knowing what's actually inside each one is comparing apples to oranges.</p>

<h2>Why cheap SEO is usually a false economy</h2>

<p>It's common to see SEO offers at a fraction of typical market rates, and it's worth understanding why those are usually not a good deal even at a lower price. The labour-intensive parts of real SEO — genuine keyword research specific to your business, unique content written for your actual services, and manual local citation building across relevant directories — are exactly the parts that get cut to hit a rock-bottom price point. What's left is often templated, automated, or outsourced work that may produce a small amount of short-term movement but rarely holds up against competitors who are investing in the real thing. In the worst cases, low-cost SEO providers rely on outdated tactics — link schemes, keyword stuffing, duplicate content across client sites — that can actively work against a site's rankings over time rather than helping them.</p>

<h2>How to evaluate an SEO quote</h2>

<p>Rather than comparing the monthly number in isolation, ask what's specifically included: Is there dedicated keyword research for your business, or a generic template applied across clients? Does the quote include content creation, or just optimization of existing pages? Is Google Business Profile management included, and does it cover ongoing posts and review management, or just the initial setup? Is local citation building included, and across how many directories? Is reporting substantive — actual ranking positions and traffic trends — or a vague summary? A lower price that's missing several of these is not actually cheaper than a higher price that includes them; it's just less SEO for less money, which is a fair trade only if that's genuinely what fits your budget and goals.</p>

<h2>One-time projects vs. ongoing retainers</h2>

<p>A one-time technical SEO audit and on-page cleanup has real, standalone value — fixing broken schema markup, cleaning up title tags and meta descriptions, resolving crawl errors, and setting a solid technical foundation. But SEO as a discipline is fundamentally ongoing: competitors keep publishing content, building links, and optimizing their own sites, and search algorithms keep evolving. Rankings built through a one-time push, without continued maintenance, tend to erode over months as competitors who kept investing pull ahead again. For most businesses, a realistic budget plan involves an initial technical foundation followed by an ongoing monthly program, rather than treating SEO as a single project with a defined end date.</p>

<h2>DIY SEO vs. hiring it out</h2>

<p>For a very small, single-location business with a limited budget, a reasonable amount of foundational SEO can be handled in-house: claiming and filling out a Google Business Profile completely, writing honest and specific page titles and descriptions, and asking happy customers directly for Google reviews all cost time rather than money, and all move the needle. Where DIY tends to fall short is content depth and consistency over time — writing genuinely useful, keyword-relevant content on a regular schedule is a real skill and a real time commitment, and it's the part most business owners end up deprioritizing once day-to-day operations get busy. Hiring this out isn't about being incapable of doing it; it's about whether it's the best use of an owner's limited hours compared to running the business itself.</p>

<h2>Contract red flags worth watching for</h2>

<p>A few patterns in SEO contracts are worth being cautious about regardless of price: guarantees of a specific ranking position (no legitimate agency controls Google's algorithm closely enough to guarantee this), long lock-in contracts with no ability to see monthly deliverables before renewing, vague reporting that shows generic "traffic" numbers without specific keyword rankings, and an unwillingness to explain, in plain terms, what work is actually being done each month. None of these automatically mean a provider is dishonest, but they're worth asking direct questions about before signing anything longer than a month-to-month agreement.</p>

<h2>Getting a number that actually fits your business</h2>

<p>Generic pricing ranges are a useful starting reference, but the number that actually matters is the one scoped to your specific industry, city or service area, and current site condition — which requires an actual look at your business, not a blanket rate. If your industry runs competitive (legal, real estate, home services in a major city) expect the higher end of typical ranges; if you're in a lower-competition market or niche, the lower end is realistic. Requesting a free audit before committing to a retainer is a reasonable ask of any agency — a real audit should be able to tell you roughly what's involved before asking for a monthly commitment.</p>
